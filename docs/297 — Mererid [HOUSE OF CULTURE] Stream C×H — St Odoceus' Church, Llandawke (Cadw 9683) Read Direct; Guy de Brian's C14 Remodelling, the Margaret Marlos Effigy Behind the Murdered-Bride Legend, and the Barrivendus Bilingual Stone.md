## Why this target

Llandawke church sits about 2.5 km west of Laugharne, inside the Laugharne Township community, and had never been object-read on this desk. It earns its place on three counts already open in the register:

1. **The de Brian link.** Both statutory records say the church was *remodelled in the fourteenth century by Sir Guy de Brian, lord marcher of Laugharne* — a rare survival of the town's greatest medieval lord in standing fabric, complementing the Complete Peerage work at HoC-288.
2. **The murdered-bride legend.** Doc 287 (Curtis 1880) logged a Laugharne "murdered bride" legend at Llandawke without its physical anchor. That anchor is the broken effigy in this church.
3. **A deep-past inscribed stone.** The church holds a 5th–6th-century bilingual Latin-and-Ogham memorial stone, sibling to the Llansadwrnen stone worked at Docs 263/264 — the same method (Coflein + the ECMW corpus) applies.

It is squarely House-of-Culture scope (township church fabric, town lord, town folklore), not Island House and not Dylan.

## The building

A small Gothic church of local red sandstone / Old Red Sandstone rubble: an **aisleless nave, a lower and narrower chancel, and a squat, tapering two-stage west tower** (Cadw 9683; plan corroborated by Coflein). The **chancel arch, nave and chancel are thirteenth-century**, with a contemporary piscina in the chancel south wall (semicircular-headed recess and bowl). The church **formerly had a rood loft** — the evidence is the surviving corbels and the blocked opening above the semicircular chancel arch, plus a thirteenth-century rood-loft doorway with a three-stepped sill.

Cadw and Coflein agree the church was **remodelled in the fourteenth century by Sir Guy de Brian, lord marcher of Laugharne**, and restored in the Victorian period. Openings are medieval, some rebuilt, in purple sandstone, limestone and yellow oolite dressings; the south entrance is a pointed, chamfered doorway with weathered spur bases.

The **tower is post-medieval, probably seventeenth-century**, and unusual for the region: it lacks the typical west door, battered base and string-course, being **battered to its full height instead**, with a spiral stair vice projecting from the north wall. The square limestone font may be post-medieval. Restoration ran into the early twentieth century; the **carved, arcaded oak altar is dated 1882** (angels, lily of the valley, rose of sharon, passion flower). Internal monuments include a mid-nineteenth-century wall tablet by **Mainwaring of Carmarthen** and two eighteenth-century floor monuments.

At Cadw's inspection in **January 1988** the church was "partly overgrown."

## The effigy and the murdered-bride legend

On the north side is a **later-fourteenth-century recumbent effigy** of a female figure — wimple, tightly folded clothes — **broken into three pieces**, recovered from the churchyard and **placed in its present position in 1902** (Cadw). Coflein records that it may originally have occupied the fourteenth-century tomb recess in the chancel west wall, and names the figure "reputedly that of St Margaret Marlo (neice of Sir Guy de Brian)."

The important distinction, held carefully:

- **Verified:** the effigy exists, is later-fourteenth-century, is broken in three, was recovered from the churchyard, and was set in place in 1902. The tomb recess (with carved head-stops and foliated apex) is real fabric.
- **Tradition:** the identification as *Margaret Marlos*, her descent as a *niece of Sir Guy de Brian*, and the story that the breakage records her being *cut into three pieces by robbers*. Cadw hedges with "probably"; Coflein with "reputedly." No source read establishes her as a historical person.

This effigy is the physical anchor of the "murdered bride" legend logged from Curtis 1880 at Doc 287. The legend now has its object; the object does not turn the legend into fact. Coflein's filing of the whole church under "St Margaret Marlos's" shows how completely the tradition has absorbed the dedication — but the church's **original dedication is to St Odoceus** (Oudoceus / Euddogwy), as the Cadw entry name still records.

## The Barrivendus stone (Stream H)

At the end of the nave stands an **Early Christian bilingual memorial stone**, formerly the church's doorstep. It is a genuine sibling to the Llansadwrnen stone (Docs 263/264) and the reading is settled across the corpus:

- **Latin, in Roman capitals, three lines (one running down the left edge):**
  **BARRIVENDI FILIVS VENDVBARI HIC IACIT** — "(the stone) of Barrivendus, son of Vendubarus; he lies here" (Nash-Williams *ECMW* 1950, 113).
- **Dating:** c.400–533 (Nash-Williams); C. Thomas 1994 narrows the Latin to c.510–530 and judges the names, "on balance," Irish, meaning "fair-haired."

A crucial nuance, easy to get wrong: the stone **also carries an Ogham inscription up its arris, and the Ogham is not a translation of the Latin.** Nash-Williams read the Ogham as *DUMELEDONAS MAQI MUCOI[…]* ("of Dumeledo, son of the kin of…"); Rhys and Macalister differ. Macalister (1945, 351) records that **the back face of the stone was split off, and with it a line of Ogham "which must have borne the name of the original owner"** — so the two inscribed texts name different people and the monument is not a clean bilingual. The Ogham may be earlier than the Latin (Thomas 1994, c.400–450).

The stone was **first noticed in 1838** (a description with a rude wood-cut, signed "A.J.K.", in the *Gentleman's Magazine*, January 1838). An incumbent later set it as the **sill of the south door**; it was moved to the vestry under the tower and, **in 1903, to its present resting place** inside the church (Treherne 1907). Placement notes disagree slightly — Cadw says "at the end of the nave", Coflein "on a ledge by the chancel arch" — a minor discrepancy, noted not reconciled.

## Patronage, and a cross-branch tie

Llandawke was **always under private patronage, under the lordship of Laugharne**; it belonged to the medieval Deanery of Stradtowy, and by 1998 to the rural deanery of St Clears (Coflein). In **1833 the living was a rectory under the patronage of William Powell, Esq.** — the same Broadway Powell family the Island House branch has been tracing (cf. IH-294, Sir John Powell of Broadway). A patronage thread worth flagging to that branch, not pursued here.

## Two claims held down

- The **local tradition that the church was founded in the fourteenth century as a monastic establishment** is logged as Tradition: Coflein states it and adds "there is no evidence to support this." The thirteenth-century chancel arch, nave and chancel predate any such foundation date in any case.
- The **999-year Friends of Friendless Churches lease from 24 April 2006** is reported only secondarily and is left **Referenced-pending** a direct read of the FoFC record. What is Verified from Coflein is that the church, then derelict, was rescued by the FoFC in 2006 and is now open to the public.
